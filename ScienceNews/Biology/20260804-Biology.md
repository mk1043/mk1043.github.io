## 內容

今天的「醫學與疾病」新聞呈現出幾個共同線索：疾病機制、臨床證據，以及潛在治療方向。這些研究或觀測不只是單一事件，而是共同指向一個趨勢：科學家正透過更細緻的資料、更精密的儀器與更跨領域的方法，重新理解自然現象背後的機制。

### 核心脈絡

從Nature的報導來看，醫學與疾病的進展通常不是靠單一突破完成，而是由觀測、實驗、模型與長期資料累積交互推動。因此，判讀今日新聞時，重點不只在於哪一項結果最吸睛，也在於它是否能補上既有理論或應用中的缺口。

### 關鍵進展

最突出的線索來自「Privacy risks from medical AI tools are not shared equally」。Nature, Published online: 04 August 2026; doi:10.1038/d41586-026-02288-9 Privacy attacks can reveal whether someone’s medical data was used to train an AI model.這類成果的價值，在於它為今日主題提供了一個可以被後續研究檢驗的切入點。與此同時，「What’s missing from the White House plan for the future of US science」、「Engineered yeast that make cancer drugs could spare a rare flower」也從不同角度補充了同一個問題：有些文章提供觀測資料，有些凸顯實驗或模型方法，另一些則指出可能的應用與風險。把這些線索放在一起看，比單獨閱讀任何一篇新聞更能看出研究方向的變化。

### 值得觀察

其中最值得後續追蹤的是「Privacy risks from medical AI tools are not shared equally」。它的意義不只在於單篇新聞本身，而在於後續是否會被更多觀測、實驗或同儕研究驗證。如果相關證據持續累積，這類成果就可能從新聞事件變成推動領域前進的穩定知識。

The use of artificial-intelligence tools in medicine has hinged on an implicit bargain. Patients and health systems permit sensitive records to be used for research, usually after de-identification so that names and other pieces of personal information are removed. This bargain relies on the assumption that it is impossible for people who have access to an AI model to infer information about individuals whose data were used to train it. A 1945 report by Vannevar Bush, director of the US Office of Scientific Research and Development, was seminal to the growth of US research. Credit: AP/Alamy Last month, Michael Kratsios, head of the US Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP), released a report called Science: A New Golden Age . The 123-page document is a response to a letter from US President Donald Trump tasking Kratsios with ushering in “a new golden age of American innovation” (see go.nature.com/8tyfk4k ). But how likely is it that the report will help the country to achieve this aim? White House rolls out AI funding — and signals a new era for US science Such a letter from the president to the OSTP head is not uncommon. It is a signal of the government’s interests and priorities. However, Trump’s letter and the response come amid a systematic effort to reshape US research. There’s a lot to take in. The report asks hard questions about the future of US science, including about how to support the research that provides the foundations for technological advances and how to enhance crucial scientific infrastructure. There are timely questions about the role of artificial intelligence in science and around the process of issuing research grants. The report proposes changes, too, to how the US federal research-funding portfolio is balanced, for instance. It addresses the persistent concern that grant managers and reviewers are too cautious, and proposes fresh ways to fund people who are willing to take risks and to individuals outside universities. The details need time for reflection and we plan to return to some of the report’s themes in greater depth in future articles. Before diving deeper into the report’s content, it’s important to consider its precedents and the context in which it was produced. A key predecessor is a 1945 report called Science: The Endless Frontier , which was submitted to then-president Harry Truman by Vannevar Bush, director of the Office of Scientific Research and Development at the time (see go.nature.com/4g7nufa ). Produced in the aftermath of the Second World War, this fundamental document in US science policy led to the establishment of a new public funding agency for basic research: the National Science Foundation. The report and its implementation took several years and was a highly inclusive exercise with inputs from academia, industry and policymakers. White House proposes vast overhaul of US science funding: what you need to know Science: A New Golden Age has also been written during a tumultuous period, but of a different kind. It was released after 18 months of executive orders , rule changes , abrupt funding cuts , delays to grants being awarded and political interference in many scientific matters that should be the domain of experts, ranging from vaccine safety to climate change . However, unlike the 1945 report, the current one does not name the members of scientific and technical communities who were consulted. This follows a pattern: Trump’s administration has substantially reduced the flow of advice from independent specialists to the federal government, as we documented in an April News Feature ( Nature 652 , 1106–1109; 2026 ). Science: A New Golden Age , at its core, is a set of priorities from Trump’s administration for scientific leaders to focus on. The fields named include “AI, quantum information science, semiconductors, advanced communications, robotics, advanced manufacturing, nuclear fission and fusion, and space systems”. Such large-scale instructions to the research community, without transparent consultation with its stakeholders, is rare for a democratic country — let alone for a powerhouse of research and the home of many globally respected universities and science-based corporations. But the details of these instructions are not straightforward to find. They are included in an annex in the main document. This part contains a memorandum for the heads of departments and executive agencies co-signed by Kratsios and Russell Vought, the director of the Office of Management and Budget. The annex includes a list of what the document calls “foundational research to drive scientific breakthroughs for emerging technologies”. The priorities focus mainly on research in physics, materials science, chemistry, computer science, engineering, space science and mathematics, and especially on “national security-relevant research portfolios”. Federal agencies with annual research budgets of at least US$3 billion have 90 days to tell the White House how they will “prioritize both the absolute level and the relative share of funding”. Furthermore, to support the administration’s priorities in biotechnology and biomanufacturing, agencies are being asked to “prioritize foundational research in the biological sciences over the life sciences, a broader category not focused on foundational research”. Details on which fields will be prioritized for funding extend over several pages. Key US science panels are being axed — and others are becoming less open But the people who can assess foundational science accurately are those closest to it: the researchers and their representative societies. The report says that parties “across the country’s scientific enterprise” have been consulted, but doesn’t specify who has provided input. Which scientists or organizations in fields such as physics, computer science, space science and chemistry were asked for feedback? Who is saying that life sciences does not focus on foundational research? One glaring omission in this proposal for overhauling science is direct evidence of input from the National Academies of Science
